An exec who has never seen a flop was never invited into the process

Source: John Cutler (@johncutlefish)
original post

What it claims

Cutler asked an executive with 20+ years of experience if they had ever seen a feature/effort “flop.” They said no. He dug. In many cases they claimed “poor execution” had left them “on their back foot.” Meanwhile new silver bullets had emerged. So they did not really know — or were not focused on — whether the original idea worked or not. In other cases they described competitive pressures or “market shifts” that again left them “late to the party.” The trend: it is all about speed and time in their mind. Perfect ideas that could not be executed on quickly enough. The interesting thing was that they had never experienced what many designers and developers have experienced: watching their amazing ideas fall flat, or cleaning up (or wading through) years of debt / non-value-producing complexity. Cutler could not really fault him, as no one had invited him into the process. The last time he had worked in the trenches it was a very different world. He was an engineer, but not in a similar context. Reminder: we are all bounded by what we have observed and know. Inviting people into the process / sharing data / watching usability tests / watching your customers use the actual product is super important. When you do, you realize how much you have to learn.

Why it matters for a PO

Leadership that has never seen a flop will diagnose every miss as execution speed or “late to the market,” then demand more silver-bullet throughput. Design and eng are left with the debt. The fix Cutler names is not a better status slide — it is inviting the exec into the process: data, usability tests, watching customers use the thing. A PO who will book that seat can replace “we were too slow” with “the idea fell flat.”

3 takeaways

  1. “Never seen a flop” usually means poor-execution / market-shift / speed stories — not evidence the idea worked.
  2. Designers and developers have watched ideas fall flat and have cleaned up years of non-value complexity; many execs have not.
  3. Invite them into the process: share data, watch usability tests, watch customers use the product.

Practice this week

For one “we were too slow” postmortem, write whether the original idea was ever checked. Invite one decision-maker to a usability session or a customer using the live product before the next “go faster” ask.
